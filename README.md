## ⚙️ How Scoring Works

The test contains **30 questions**:

- Energy and attention: 8 questions
- Information: 8 questions
- Decision-making: 7 questions
- Approach to plans: 7 questions

For each question, select the statement that best reflects your usual preference. Option A contributes to E, S, T, or J, while Option B contributes to I, N, F, or P.

The application counts the selected preferences in each dimension and combines the winning letters into a four-letter result, such as `INTJ` or `ENFP`. If a dimension is tied, the first-listed preference (E, S, T, or J) is selected.

Results are intended for self-reflection, not definitive personality classification.

## What Is MBTI?

The Myers-Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI) is a personality framework that describes preferences across four dimensions. Combining the preferences produces one of 16 four-letter personality types.

For example, a person might receive a result such as `INTJ`, `ENFP`, or `ISFJ`.

Each letter represents one preference from a pair of alternatives:

- **E or I:** Extraversion or Introversion
- **S or N:** Sensing or Intuition
- **T or F:** Thinking or Feeling
- **J or P:** Judging or Perceiving

These preferences describe different tendencies in how individuals may direct their attention, take in information, make decisions, and approach the outside world.

However, people are complex. Personality cannot be fully represented by four letters, and an individual's behaviour can change depending on the situation, experience, environment, and personal development.

This self-test uses the MBTI framework as a guide for reflection. It does not establish an official or scientifically definitive personality classification.

## The Four Personality Dimensions

### 1. Extraversion (E) vs. Introversion (I)

This dimension concerns where people tend to direct their attention and how they may prefer to engage with their environment.

**Extraversion (E)**

People who prefer extraversion may tend to:

- Engage actively with people and their surroundings.
- Enjoy discussing ideas with others.
- Think aloud while exploring a problem.
- Gain energy from social interaction.

**Introversion (I)**

People who prefer introversion may tend to:

- Reflect internally before sharing their thoughts.
- Prefer quieter environments for concentration.
- Enjoy independent work or deeper conversations.
- Appreciate time alone to process experiences.

Neither preference means that a person is always outgoing or always shy. Introverted people can be effective speakers, and extraverted people can enjoy working independently.

### 2. Sensing (S) vs. Intuition (N)

This dimension concerns how people tend to notice and interpret information.

**Sensing (S)**

People who prefer sensing may tend to:

- Focus on facts, details, and observable information.
- Prefer practical examples and clear instructions.
- Apply established methods to solve problems.
- Pay attention to present realities and experience.

**Intuition (N)**

People who prefer intuition may tend to:

- Explore patterns, possibilities, and connections.
- Enjoy abstract concepts and theoretical discussions.
- Consider future opportunities and potential outcomes.
- Look for alternative ways to understand a problem.

Both preferences are valuable. Effective problem-solving often requires attention to concrete details as well as broader possibilities.

### 3. Thinking (T) vs. Feeling (F)

This dimension concerns preferences in evaluating information and making decisions.

**Thinking (T)**

People who prefer thinking may tend to:

- Emphasize logical analysis and consistency.
- Compare alternatives using objective criteria.
- Examine causes, consequences, and evidence.
- Prioritize fairness through consistent principles.

**Feeling (F)**

People who prefer feeling may tend to:

- Consider personal values and human impact.
- Pay attention to relationships and group needs.
- Evaluate how decisions affect different people.
- Seek outcomes that align with important values.

These preferences do not determine whether someone is intelligent, rational, caring, or emotional. People with either preference can demonstrate strong analytical skills and empathy.

### 4. Judging (J) vs. Perceiving (P)

This dimension concerns how people may prefer to approach structure, planning, and the outside world.

**Judging (J)**

People who prefer judging may tend to:

- Appreciate plans, schedules, and clear deadlines.
- Prefer organizing tasks before starting.
- Work toward decisions and closure.
- Feel comfortable when expectations are established.

**Perceiving (P)**

People who prefer perceiving may tend to:

- Prefer flexibility and keeping options open.
- Adapt their approach as new information emerges.
- Explore alternatives before making decisions.
- Enjoy spontaneous activities or evolving plans.

Neither preference is inherently better. Successful projects often require both structured planning and the flexibility to adapt.

The four preference dimensions combine to produce 16 possible personality types. The descriptions below are broad introductory summaries, not rules that every person with a particular type must follow.

## How to Take the Test

Follow these steps to complete the self-test.

### Step 1: Open the Website 

Visit the published website https://jyhtonix.github.io/mbti-personality-compass/ using a supported web browser, such as Google Chrome, Microsoft Edge, Mozilla Firefox, or Safari.

### Step 2: Enter Your Information

If the application requests your name, enter it as instructed. Provide only the information necessary to use the application.

### Step 3: Read Each Question Carefully

Consider what you would naturally prefer in ordinary situations rather than what you believe is expected of you.

### Step 4: Answer Honestly

Choose the answer that most closely reflects your usual preference.

There are no inherently right or wrong answers. Avoid selecting an option simply because it appears more desirable, professional, or socially acceptable.

### Step 5: Complete All Questions

Answer the entire questionnaire as instructed by the application. Take your time, but avoid overthinking every response.

### Step 6: Review Your Results

Read the personality type and accompanying description carefully. Consider which observations seem relevant to you and which do not.

### Step 7: Reflect on Your Findings

Ask yourself:

- Does this result reflect how I usually behave?
- Which descriptions seem accurate?
- Which descriptions do not fit me?
- How might my preferences affect teamwork or communication?
- What skills or behaviours would I like to develop?

The value of the exercise lies in reflection, not simply in obtaining a four-letter result.

## Understanding Your Results

Your result represents the outcome of the questionnaire's scoring method. It should be understood as an indication of preferences based on your answers, rather than a permanent label.

### A personality type is not your identity

You are more than four letters. Your knowledge, skills, values, culture, experiences, relationships, and circumstances all contribute to who you are.

### Preferences are not abilities

A preference does not indicate that you lack the ability to perform tasks associated with the alternative preference.

For example:

- An introverted person can be an excellent presenter.
- An extraverted person can be an effective independent researcher.
- A thinking-preference individual can be compassionate.
- A feeling-preference individual can be highly analytical.
- A judging-preference individual can adapt to change.
- A perceiving-preference individual can manage projects effectively.

### Your result may change

Your responses may vary as you gain experience, enter new environments, or understand yourself better. Some people may also find that they relate closely to both sides of a dimension.

This does not necessarily mean that something is wrong with you. Human personality and behaviour are more nuanced than a simplified classification system can capture.

### Use your results as a conversation starter

Rather than asking, "What can people of my type do?", consider asking, "What can I learn about myself, and how can I use that knowledge constructively?"

## Educational and Personal Development Applications

The self-test may be used as an introductory activity in educational settings, personal development sessions, and team discussions.

### For Students

Students can use the results to reflect on:

- Individual learning and study preferences.
- Communication during group assignments.
- Approaches to problem-solving.
- Collaboration with classmates who have different perspectives.
- Personal strengths and areas for improvement.

### For Educators

Educators may use the activity to introduce discussions about:

- Individual differences in learning and communication.
- Respectful collaboration in diverse classrooms.
- Reflection and metacognitive learning.
- Teamwork and interpersonal awareness.
- The importance of avoiding assumptions about others.

The results should not be used to assign students to fixed learning styles or restrict their learning opportunities.

### For Teams

Team members may use the framework to discuss how they prefer to plan, communicate, make decisions, and respond to uncertainty.

A productive discussion focuses on how team members can work together more effectively, rather than categorizing colleagues or assuming that their behaviour can be predicted by their personality type.

### For Lifelong Learners

Self-awareness is an ongoing process. Use the test as one of many opportunities to reflect, receive feedback, explore new perspectives, and develop practical skills.
